Brake Line Construction and Flare Types

Brake lines carry hydraulic fluid under high pressure (up to 2,000 PSI during hard stops) to every wheel. They must be rigid, corrosion-resistant, and properly flared at every connection.

Materials:

  • Steel (double-wall welded): Standard OEM material. Prone to corrosion in the rust belt. Must be replaced — never repaired with compression fittings.
  • Nickel-copper (NiCopp): More corrosion-resistant than steel. Easier to bend. Accepted by most OEMs for repair.
  • Stainless steel: Maximum corrosion resistance. Used in performance applications. Requires special flaring tools.

Flare types — critical to get right:

TypeShapeUsed On
Double Inverted FlareTwo-step flare, folded inwardMost domestic vehicles — most common
ISO / Bubble FlareSingle bubble shapeMost import/European vehicles
SAE 45° FlareSingle 45° coneNOT approved for brake lines — fuel/oil only
Using the wrong flare type or a SAE 45° flare on a brake line is a life-safety defect. The fitting will appear to seal initially but will fail under brake pressure. Always confirm the correct flare type before fabricating replacement lines.

Rubber Flex Hoses

Flexible rubber hoses connect the fixed steel lines to the moving suspension/steering components. They allow the steering and suspension to move without kinking or pulling the rigid lines.

Each vehicle typically has 3 flex hoses: one per front wheel and one center rear (or one per rear wheel on independent rear suspension).

Flex hose failure modes:

  • External cracking: Rubber deteriorates with age and ozone exposure — cracks become pressure leaks
  • Internal delamination: Inner lining separates and acts as a one-way check valve — pressure goes in but cannot return. Brakes apply but do not release. Caliper drags and overheats.
  • Swelling / soft spots: Pressure bulge visible when brakes are applied — replace immediately
Internal delamination is invisible from the outside. If a caliper drags after installing new pads and the guide pins are not seized, suspect the flex hose. Crack the bleeder — if the wheel turns freely after opening the bleeder, the flex hose is trapping pressure.

Brake Line Inspection and Replacement

Inspection points (every brake job):

  • Check all flex hoses for cracking, swelling, or leaks — flex them slightly by hand
  • Inspect steel lines at chassis attachment points and near fuel/exhaust heat sources — corrosion concentrates here
  • Check all fittings for weeping — even small seeps must be repaired, not monitored
  • Check hose routing — no rubbing on suspension, exhaust, or frame sharp edges

Replacement rules:

  • Never use compression fittings on brake lines — not approved for hydraulic brakes
  • Always use the correct flare type for the vehicle
  • Tube bender and flaring tool are required — hand bending creates kinks and stress points
  • After replacing any line or hose, bleed the system completely before returning the vehicle
When replacing corroded rear brake lines on high-mileage vehicles, it is often more efficient to replace the entire rear section as a pre-bent kit rather than fabricating individual segments.
