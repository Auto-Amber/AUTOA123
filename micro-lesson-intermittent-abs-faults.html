Why ABS Faults Are Tough

The hardest ABS faults are the intermittent ones. The light comes on, the customer brings it in, the light goes out, and the codes might even self-clear after a few drive cycles.

Most intermittent ABS faults are caused by issues that only show up under specific conditions: bumps, turns, water on the road, heat, vibration, or specific speeds.

You can't just plug in a scan tool, read codes, replace what the code points to, and call it done. You have to chase the actual physical cause.

Slide 2 of 3

Common Culprits

The usual suspects for ABS phantom faults:

  • Wiring damage: ABS sensor wires run along the suspension, which moves constantly. Look for chafed, melted, or torn insulation. Common at flex points and where wires pass through brackets.
  • Tone ring damage: Bent, chipped, or rusted tone ring teeth produce inconsistent signal. Tone rings can also crack and separate, especially press-fit rings on bearing hubs.
  • Connector problems: Water intrusion, corrosion, or backed-out terminals. Most ABS connectors have a weather seal, but seals fail over time.
  • Bearing problems: A loose wheel bearing changes the air gap between the sensor and the tone ring, producing weak or erratic signal.
Slide 3 of 3

Systematic Diagnosis

Steps to actually find an intermittent ABS fault:

  • Look at freeze frame data: Was the fault at a specific speed? Was it after a long drive (heat)? Was it during a turn?
  • Wiggle test with scan tool live data: Watch the wheel speed PIDs while wiggling the harness, pulling on connectors, turning the wheel through full lock. Any drop or spike points to the bad spot.
  • Inspect the tone ring with a flashlight: Look for damage, debris between teeth, or signs the ring has shifted on the hub.
  • Check the bearing for play: Loose bearings cause sensor air gap issues. Often missed.
  • Replicate the conditions: If the fault only shows up during specific driving, take the vehicle out and replicate them with the scan tool data recording.
Resist the urge to replace the wheel speed sensor first. It's the most common assumption, but often the sensor is fine and something else is causing the signal problem.
