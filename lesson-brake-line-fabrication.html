Material Matters

Brake lines carry high-pressure hydraulic fluid. The line material has to handle that pressure for the life of the vehicle:

  • OE replacement: coated steel or NiCopp (copper-nickel alloy). Approved for use.
  • Stainless steel: approved, but harder to flare cleanly.
  • Pure copper: NOT approved for brake lines. Work-hardens and cracks under vibration.
NiCopp lines are the modern favorite: easy to bend, easy to flare, corrosion resistant. DOT approved for use as a steel brake line replacement.
Slide 2 of 3

Flare Types

Two flare types you'll encounter on automotive brake lines:

  • Double flare (45 degree, inverted): the line is folded back on itself before being flared. Most common in domestic and Asian vehicles.
  • ISO bubble flare: rounded bubble on the end of the line. Common in European vehicles.
A single flare alone is never acceptable on a brake line. Single flares can split under the pressure pulses of normal braking. Always double or bubble flare.

Fabrication Best Practices

Quick checklist for fabricating brake lines:

  • Cut square. Tubing cutter, not a hacksaw.
  • Deburr inside and out. Burrs prevent a clean flare and damage seats.
  • Slide the fitting on the line BEFORE flaring. Easy mistake to make.
  • Match the flare type to the original line. Don't mix bubble and double flares.
  • Bend with a tubing bender. Kinks restrict flow and weaken the line.
