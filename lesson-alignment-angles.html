Caster and Camber

Three primary angles define wheel alignment: caster, camber, and toe. Each affects a different aspect of vehicle behavior. Understanding what each angle does -- not just its number -- is what makes you useful during an alignment diagnosis.

Caster is the tilt of the steering axis as viewed from the side of the vehicle. Positive caster means the top of the steering axis leans toward the rear of the vehicle. Positive caster creates directional stability (the vehicle tends to track straight) and causes the wheel to return to center after a turn. Most vehicles run 3-8 degrees positive caster. A side-to-side caster split causes pull toward the side with less positive caster.

Caster is usually not adjustable on most front-wheel-drive vehicles. If caster is out of spec, it typically indicates a bent or shifted component -- subframe, strut tower, or control arm. Caster changes after a collision are a red flag.

Camber is the tilt of the wheel as viewed from the front. Zero camber = perfectly vertical. Negative camber = top of the tire tilts inward. Positive camber = top tilts outward. Excessive negative camber causes inner tire wear. Excessive positive causes outer wear. A side-to-side camber split causes pull toward the side with more positive (or less negative) camber.

Toe -- The Most Critical Angle

Toe is the most important alignment angle for tire wear. It describes whether the fronts of the tires point inward (toe-in) or outward (toe-out) as viewed from above. Toe is measured in degrees or inches/millimeters.

Why toe is critical: Even a small toe error causes rapid, feathered tire wear because the tire is constantly scrubbing sideways as it rolls forward. A vehicle with 1/4 inch of toe-out will wear a front tire to the cords in 10,000 miles.

Feathered wear (edges of tread blocks worn on one side, sharp on the other) is the signature of a toe problem. Run your hand across the tread to feel it -- like a saw blade in one direction, smooth the other.

Thrust Angle and Rear Alignment

On a four-wheel alignment, the rear wheels matter as much as the front. The thrust angle is the direction the rear axle is actually pointed relative to the vehicle centerline. If the rear axle is not centered, the vehicle will dog-track (crab walk) -- the front and rear tracks are offset from each other.

Dog-track diagnosis: Look at the vehicle from behind while it drives away from you on a straight road. If the rear of the vehicle is offset to one side, the thrust angle is out. The vehicle will steer with the wheel off-center to compensate, and the driver feels like the car is constantly being pushed sideways.

On vehicles with adjustable rear toe (most independent rear suspension), the thrust angle is corrected by adjusting rear toe equally left and right. On solid rear axle vehicles, a thrust angle problem usually means the axle housing has shifted or the leaf spring perch has rotated.

Alignment order: Always set rear alignment before front. The front alignment is referenced to the rear thrust line. Doing the front first and then adjusting the rear will require doing the front again.
