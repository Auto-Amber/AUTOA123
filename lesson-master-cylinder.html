What the Master Cylinder Does

The master cylinder is the hydraulic pump of the braking system. When you press the brake pedal, it converts mechanical force into hydraulic pressure that travels through the brake lines to all four corners.

Modern vehicles use a tandem (dual) master cylinder — two pistons in one bore, operating independently:

  • Primary piston: pushed directly by the booster pushrod
  • Secondary piston: pushed by the primary hydraulic pressure
  • Each piston serves a separate hydraulic circuit — split diagonal or front/rear
The tandem design is a safety feature. If one circuit fails (a leak), the other circuit still provides braking. You will have higher pedal travel but the vehicle will still stop.

The master cylinder body is made of cast iron or aluminum and is mounted directly to the brake booster (or firewall on vehicles without a booster).

Reservoir, Ports, and Fluid Path

The master cylinder has a fluid reservoir on top, divided into two sections — one for each circuit. It keeps the system topped off and allows for fluid expansion.

Two critical ports in each bore:

Compensating Port (large)
Located near the piston at rest. Allows fluid to return to the reservoir. Must be open at rest — if blocked by a pushed-in piston, brakes will drag and overheat.
Replenishing Port (small)
Prevents vacuum lock on return stroke. Allows fluid in as pistons retract. Keeps system full.

When the piston moves forward past the compensating port, pressure builds in the circuit. When pedal is released, piston returns and the port opens — pressure drops and brakes release.

Swollen or damaged seals can block the compensating port, causing brake drag that generates heat and can lead to brake fade or fire.

Master Cylinder Failure and Testing

Master cylinders fail in two primary ways:

  • External leak: Fluid leaks past the primary seal to the outside — visible fluid, low reservoir level
  • Internal bypass: Fluid leaks past a worn piston seal internally — no external leak, but pedal sinks under steady pressure

The pedal sink test: With the engine running and brakes applied firmly, hold steady pressure for 30 seconds. If the pedal slowly sinks toward the floor — internal bypass is the likely cause.

Internal bypass is a common failure mode and is easy to miss because there is no visible fluid. The pedal feel during normal stopping may seem normal — the fault shows under sustained load.

Pushrod adjustment matters: The booster-to-master cylinder pushrod must be adjusted to spec. Too long = compensating port blocked at rest = brake drag. Too short = excess pedal travel before braking begins.
