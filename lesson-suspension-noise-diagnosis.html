Why It Matters

"There's a noise from the front end" is one of the most common customer complaints. The noise itself tells you a lot if you know what to listen for.

Suspension noises break into a few categories based on WHEN they happen:

  • Over bumps - usually a joint or bushing
  • While turning - usually a bearing, joint, or CV component
  • At highway speed - usually a bearing or tire issue
  • Under braking - usually a strut, mount, or brake-related
Slide 2 of 3
Common Sounds and Sources

Match the sound to the part:

  • Clunk over bumps - sway bar end link, ball joint, control arm bushing, strut mount
  • Hum/growl that changes with vehicle speed - wheel bearing
  • Click when turning - worn CV joint (outer)
  • Grind when turning - upper strut bearing
  • Squeak over slow bumps - dry control arm bushing or sway bar bushing
  • Pop or knock under acceleration - inner CV joint or motor mount
Test order

Always road test first, then dry park test, then visual inspection. Most noises reveal their source if you systematically work through these.

Slide 3 of 3
When the Sound Lies

Watch for these tricky situations:

  • A collapsed motor mount can sound exactly like a worn ball joint
  • A loose exhaust hanger can sound like a sway bar end link
  • Worn brake hardware (clips, abutment shims) can mimic suspension clunks
  • Worn shocks/struts can amplify other noises by letting components bottom out
Diagnostic discipline

Always check the mounts (engine, trans, body). Always check the exhaust. Always check the brakes. Don't condemn a suspension part until you've ruled out look-alikes.

Knowledge Check - Question 1 of 3
Unusual noise during a test drive can be caused by:
  • Defective wheel bearing or stabilizer bar end link
  • Defective control arm bushing or ball joint
  • Worn CV joint
  • All of the above
Correct. Suspension noise has many sources. Wheel bearings hum/growl, sway bar end links clunk in turns, control arm bushings squeak or thud, ball joints clunk over bumps, and worn CV joints click in turns. Always road test + dry park + inspect to isolate.
Not quite. All of these can cause noise. That's the whole point - you need a systematic diagnosis (road test, dry park, visual inspection) because so many components can produce similar sounds.
Knowledge Check - Question 2 of 3
A customer complains of a hum that gets louder as the vehicle goes faster, and the pitch changes when they steer slightly. What is the most likely cause?
  • Worn shock absorber
  • Worn wheel bearing
  • Loose ball joint
  • Failed sway bar bushing
Correct. A hum that changes with speed AND changes with steering input (load shift) is the classic wheel bearing signature. Steering loads or unloads the bearing, changing the noise.
Not quite. Wheel bearings hum, and the hum changes when you steer because steering shifts the load on the bearing. Shocks bounce, ball joints clunk, sway bar bushings squeak. Only bearings produce a speed-related hum.
Knowledge Check - Question 3 of 3
What is the FIRST thing to do when a customer reports a suspension noise?
  • Replace the most common worn part
  • Quote the customer a complete suspension overhaul
  • Road test the vehicle to recreate and identify the noise
  • Check the air filter
Correct. Always road test FIRST. Recreate the noise. Understand when it happens. Then move to the lift for a dry park test and visual inspection. Diagnose before you replace.
Not quite. Diagnosis comes first. Road test to identify the noise and the conditions. Replacing parts without diagnosing leads to comebacks and unhappy customers.
