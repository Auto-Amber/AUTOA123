How Rack and Pinion Works

The rack and pinion steering gear is the most common steering gear on modern vehicles. The pinion is a small gear on the end of the steering shaft. The rack is a toothed bar that slides left and right as the pinion turns. Tie rods connect each end of the rack to the steering knuckle.

Advantages over recirculating ball: Fewer joints, more direct driver feel, lighter weight, and simpler construction. Recirculating ball steering gears are now found mainly on light trucks and older vehicles.

Inner tie rod ends (sockets) are threaded into the ends of the rack and connect to the outer tie rod ends. They allow the tie rod to pivot as the suspension moves up and down while still transmitting steering force. Inner tie rod failures cause a clunking noise during steering and steering wander -- the inner socket can be felt moving loosely by grabbing the tie rod while a helper turns the wheel.

Bellows boots: The accordion boots at each end of the rack protect the inner tie rod sockets and the rack from dirt and moisture. A torn boot quickly leads to socket wear and corrosion of the rack surface. Always inspect boots during any steering service.

Power Steering -- Hydraulic and Electric

Manual rack and pinion requires significant steering effort at low speeds. Power assist reduces effort by adding force to the rack movement.

Hydraulic power steering (HPS): An engine-driven pump pressurizes power steering fluid. A control valve in the steering gear directs this pressure to assist in the direction the driver is turning. The system is always on -- the pump runs even when driving straight, consuming engine power.

Electric power steering (EPS): An electric motor provides assist, either on the column (column-assist), on the rack (rack-assist), or on the pinion. The module decides how much assist to provide based on vehicle speed, steering angle rate, and torque sensor input.

EPS Initialization and Diagnosis

EPS systems require a scan tool for diagnosis and often require an initialization (zero-point calibration) procedure after repairs that disturb the steering angle sensor -- alignment, steering gear replacement, or steering column work.

When initialization is required:
-- Steering gear replacement
-- Steering angle sensor replacement
-- Four-wheel alignment (on vehicles where the steering angle sensor self-zeros on a straight road, this may happen automatically, but confirm with the service info)
-- Battery disconnect on some vehicles (the module loses its learned center point)

EPS diagnostic approach:

  1. Retrieve all DTCs first -- EPS DTCs often point directly to the faulty sensor or circuit.
  2. Check for power, ground, and CAN bus communication at the EPS module.
  3. Look up torque sensor and steering angle sensor data in live data to verify signals.
  4. Perform initialization/zero-point calibration procedure from the service manual if required.
