Stabilizer Bars
How sway bars resist body roll in corners

What It Does

The stabilizer bar (also called a sway bar or anti-roll bar) is a torsion spring that connects the left and right suspension on the same axle.

When both wheels move together (over a bump), the bar rotates as a unit and does nothing. But when one wheel moves and the other doesn't (body roll in a corner), the bar twists - and that twist resists the motion.

In a corner

The outside suspension compresses, the inside extends. The bar links them. The twist resistance reduces how far the body leans, keeping the vehicle flatter through the turn.

Slide 2 of 3
Components

A typical stabilizer bar setup has these parts:

  • The bar itself - hollow or solid steel, often coated
  • Frame bushings - hold the bar to the chassis, allow it to rotate
  • End links - connect each end of the bar to the suspension (usually the lower control arm or strut)
  • End link bushings - allow articulation at the connections

The end links are the most common failure point. They take repeated bending and the bushings wear out.

Diagnosing Sway Bar Issues

Common symptoms:

  • Clunk on bumps - usually a loose or cracked end link, or worn bushings
  • Excessive body roll - broken bar, missing bar, or completely worn bushings
  • Rattling over rough roads - loose end link, missing bushing
Test it

With the vehicle on a hoist, grab the end link and try to wiggle it. Significant play = bushing or joint wear. Listen and feel for clicks. Compare both sides.

Knowledge Check - Question 1 of 3
Which suspension component counteracts body lean during cornering?
  • Control arm
  • Strut
  • Stabilizer bar
  • Torsion bar
Correct. The stabilizer (sway) bar twists when one wheel moves differently from the other - that twist resists body roll in corners.
Not quite. Stabilizer bars connect left to right and resist body roll specifically. Control arms locate the wheel. Struts handle vertical motion. Torsion bars work as primary springs.
Knowledge Check - Question 2 of 3
A customer complains of a 'clunk' when driving over bumps. The vehicle drives straight. What suspension component is most likely worn?
  • The coil spring
  • The wheel bearing
  • A stabilizer bar end link
  • The shock absorber
Correct. End links are the most common source of clunking over bumps. The bushings wear, the ball stud loosens, and the link makes noise on every bump.
Not quite. Springs don't clunk. Bearings hum or growl. Shocks usually clunk only when seriously damaged. End links are the #1 source of bump-clunk noise.
Knowledge Check - Question 3 of 3
What is the function of the stabilizer bar when both wheels hit a bump at the same time?
  • It maximizes the suspension travel
  • Nothing - it just rotates as a unit and does not twist
  • It absorbs the impact like a third spring
  • It locks the suspension to prevent motion
Correct. When both wheels move equally, the bar rotates as a single unit with no twist - so it adds no resistance. Only differential motion (one wheel up, one not) makes it work.
Not quite. The bar only resists DIFFERENTIAL motion between sides. Equal compression on both sides rotates the bar without twisting it, so it doesn't do anything in that case.
