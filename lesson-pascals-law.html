Pascal's Law — The Foundation of Hydraulic Brakes

Pascal's Law states: pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally in all directions throughout the fluid.

This is why hydraulic brakes work. One master cylinder can simultaneously apply equal pressure to four brake calipers or wheel cylinders at completely different locations — all from one pedal push.

Pressure formula:
Pressure (PSI) = Force (lbs) ÷ Area (in²)

If you apply 100 lbs of force to a master cylinder piston with 1 in² area: pressure = 100 PSI throughout the entire system.

That same 100 PSI at a front caliper with a 3 in² piston creates 300 lbs of clamping force — 3× the input force. This is hydraulic force multiplication.

Force Multiplication and Brake Sizing

Hydraulic brakes use different piston sizes to apply different forces at each wheel. Larger pistons receive more force from the same pressure.

Front brakes do more work than rear brakes (weight transfer under braking), so front calipers have larger pistons than rear calipers on most vehicles.

Master cylinder
Small bore = high pressure from moderate force
Typical bore: 7/8" to 1"
Rear caliper / wheel cylinder
Smaller piston = moderate clamping force
Typical: 1.5"–1.75" bore
Front caliper (disc)
Larger piston(s) = more clamping force
Typical: 1.75"–2.5" bore (or dual pistons)
Wheel cylinder (drum)
Two pistons push outward on brake shoes
Typical: 3/4" to 1" bore

Brake bias — the proportion of front vs. rear braking force — is engineered into piston sizes, caliper placement, and proportioning valves.

Why Air in the System Destroys Hydraulic Function

Hydraulic systems work because liquid is incompressible. When you push on brake fluid, the force transfers instantly and completely to the pistons at the other end.

Air is compressible. When air gets into a brake line:

  • Pedal input energy first compresses the air bubble instead of moving the piston
  • Pedal travel increases significantly before any hydraulic pressure builds
  • Result: spongy, low pedal — the classic sign of air in the system
  • Severe cases: pedal goes to the floor with little or no braking effect
A spongy brake pedal is a safety emergency. Do not drive the vehicle until the brake system has been bled and the air source identified and corrected.

Why bleeding removes air: Brake fluid (liquid) follows gravity and flows out of the bleeder valve at the caliper. Air bubbles rise and are pushed out with the fluid during the bleeding procedure.
