Power Steering Fluids and Why They Matter

Power steering fluid is a hydraulic fluid that also lubricates the pump, seals, and valve in the steering gear. Using the wrong fluid is one of the most common causes of premature pump and seal failure in the field.

Never use ATF as a universal substitute. Some older vehicles specified ATF; many modern systems require a specific OEM power steering fluid or a vehicle-specific synthetic. ATF in the wrong system damages seals within weeks. Always check the reservoir cap or ShopKey for the exact specification.
Fluid typeApplicationKey distinction
OEM-specific PS fluidMost modern vehiclesFormulated for that vehicle's seals and pump -- check the reservoir cap marking
ATF (Dexron/Mercon)Some older domestic vehiclesOnly where specifically called for -- mixing with modern systems damages seals
Synthetic PS fluidEuropean vehicles, some importOften CHF 11S (BMW, Mercedes, VW/Audi) -- not interchangeable with standard PS fluid
EPS vehiclesElectric power steeringNo hydraulic fluid at all -- do not add fluid to an EPS reservoir (there is none)
Fluid condition: Dark brown or black fluid, foam, milky appearance, or a burnt smell all indicate the fluid needs replacement. Normal PS fluid is clear, amber, or light red depending on type.

Power Steering Flush and Bleed Procedure

Power steering fluid degrades over time -- it absorbs moisture, breaks down chemically, and picks up metal particles from pump and valve wear. Flushing the system is recommended at manufacturer-specified intervals and any time the pump, gear, or hoses are replaced.

Flush procedure (turkey baster/drain method):
1. Remove as much old fluid from the reservoir as possible using a turkey baster or fluid evacuator.
2. Refill with fresh fluid of the correct type.
3. Start the engine and cycle the steering lock to lock several times to circulate the new fluid.
4. Repeat the evacuate-and-refill process 2-3 times until the fluid coming out matches the fluid going in.
5. Top off to the correct level and verify no air in the system (foamy fluid = air).

Bleeding after repair (component replacement):

  • Fill the reservoir, start the engine, and cycle the wheel lock to lock slowly several times.
  • Check and refill the reservoir frequently -- air pockets come out and the level drops.
  • A groaning or whining noise that goes away after cycling indicates air that is still being purged.
  • Check under the vehicle for leaks during and after the bleed procedure.

Leak Diagnosis and Pump Testing

Power steering leaks are common and easy to misdiagnose -- fluid can travel along hoses and drip far from the actual source. Always clean the area thoroughly and use UV dye or chalk powder to pinpoint the origin.

Leak locationLikely source
Near the pump pulleyPump front shaft seal
At high/low pressure hose connectionsO-rings, flare fittings, or crimped hose end failures
At the steering gear/rackInput shaft seal, rack seals (inner), or output shaft seal
At the reservoirReturn line O-ring, reservoir cap seal, or cracked reservoir

Pump pressure test: A power steering pressure test uses a gauge set with a shutoff valve in series with the high-pressure hose. With the engine running, closing the valve momentarily measures relief pressure (should reach 1,000-1,500 psi depending on spec). Opening the valve and turning lock to lock measures assist pressure. A pump that cannot reach spec is worn and should be replaced.

Never close the test valve for more than 5 seconds -- the fluid heats rapidly with no place to flow and can damage pump seals.
