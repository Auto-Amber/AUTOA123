How non-coil springs work, ride height adjustment, and service considerations

Torsion Bars -- Springs That Twist

A torsion bar is a straight steel bar that acts as a spring by twisting along its length. One end is anchored to the vehicle frame; the other end connects to the lower control arm. When the wheel hits a bump and the control arm moves up, the bar twists -- and that resistance to twisting is what supports the vehicle's weight.

Key advantage over coil springs: Torsion bar ride height is adjustable. Most torsion bar vehicles have an adjustment bolt at the frame anchor that increases or decreases preload on the bar, raising or lowering that corner of the vehicle. This makes torsion bar systems popular on 4x4 trucks where leveling for accessory weight is common.

Common torsion bar applications include the Dodge Ram, GM full-size trucks, and many Chrysler vehicles from the 1980s through 2000s. Some Toyota 4Runner and Tacoma models also used torsion bars front.

Service note: Torsion bars are under significant preload. Never remove a torsion bar without first relieving the preload using the adjustment bolt -- unwinding it until spring tension is released. Releasing a loaded torsion bar unexpectedly can cause serious injury. Always look up the procedure in ShopKey before beginning.

Leaf Springs -- Layers That Work Together

A leaf spring is a stack of progressively shorter steel strips (leaves) clamped together at the center. The spring arches between two mounting points: the front eye is fixed to the frame, and the rear shackle allows the spring to change length as it flattens under load.

Leaf springs are found primarily on rear axles of trucks and vans, and on some older rear-wheel-drive cars. They are simple, durable, and handle heavy loads well. The shackle is a critical wear point -- a worn or seized shackle bushing causes handling problems and noise.

Multi-leaf vs mono-leaf: Traditional multi-leaf stacks use inter-leaf friction for damping (adds harshness). Modern mono-leaf or two-leaf composite springs reduce weight and improve ride but cannot be stacked -- replace as a unit.

Inspection and Common Failure Points

Both spring types share a common failure pattern: fatigue. Springs lose rate and ride height over time and eventually crack. Ride height is the first indicator of spring health.

ComponentWhat to checkFailure symptom
Torsion barAdjustment bolt, anchor bracket, bar for cracks at hex endLow ride height one side, clunking at anchor
Leaf spring leavesCracked or broken leaves (esp. main leaf), center bolt shear, leaf separationAxle shift, vehicle leans, clunk over bumps
Leaf spring eye bushingsRubber cracked, collapsed, or extrudedClunking, wandering, tire wear
Rear shackle bushingsPlay in shackle, bushing conditionClunking, spring angle change affecting handling
U-bolt clampsTorque, corrosion, axle centered on spring padAxle wrap, vibration under acceleration
A broken main leaf is a safety failure -- do not drive the vehicle. The main leaf is what retains the axle in position. If it fractures, the axle can rotate under the vehicle and cause loss of control.
