MacPherson Strut Suspension
The most common front suspension on modern cars

What It Is

The MacPherson strut combines the shock absorber, coil spring, and upper suspension mount into one tall, vertical assembly.

Compared to a short-and-long-arm (SLA) setup, a MacPherson strut uses:

  • One control arm per side (the lower one only)
  • One ball joint per side (only the lower)
  • One strut assembly doing the job of an upper arm + shock + spring
Why engineers love it

Fewer parts. Compact. Lower cost. Frees up engine bay space - important for transverse-engine front-wheel-drive layouts.

Slide 2 of 3
How It Steers

On a MacPherson, the entire strut assembly rotates when you turn the wheel.

The strut is connected at the top through a strut bearing mount - a thrust bearing that lets the whole strut spin in its top mount. The bottom of the strut is bolted rigidly to the steering knuckle, so when the knuckle steers, the strut goes with it.

That means the upper strut mount has to handle:

  • Vertical loads (vehicle weight)
  • Side loads (cornering forces)
  • Rotational motion (steering)
Slide 3 of 3
Service Implications

Because the MacPherson strut combines so many parts into one assembly:

  • You almost always replace it as a unit ("loaded strut")
  • Removing the spring from a strut requires a spring compressor - DANGEROUS if done wrong
  • A worn upper strut bearing causes a clicking or grinding sound when steering
  • Strut replacement changes wheel alignment - always realign after
Spring safety

NEVER disassemble a strut without a proper, rated spring compressor. The coil spring stores enough energy to be fatal if it releases suddenly.

Knowledge Check - Question 1 of 3
Two technicians are discussing MacPherson struts. Tech A says the entire strut assembly rotates when the wheels are turned. Tech B says a MacPherson suspension uses only one control arm and one ball joint per side. Who is correct?
  • Tech A only
  • Tech B only
  • Both technicians
  • Neither technician
Correct. Both are correct. The strut rotates as a unit on its upper bearing when steered, and a MacPherson uses only a lower control arm and lower ball joint per side - no upper arm.
Not quite. Both statements are true. The strut DOES rotate when steering (supported by the upper bearing), AND a MacPherson uses only ONE control arm and ONE ball joint per side. Each tech is talking about a different correct attribute.
Knowledge Check - Question 2 of 3
A customer complains of a clicking or grinding noise when turning the steering wheel, especially at low speeds. What MacPherson strut component is most likely worn?
  • The lower ball joint
  • The coil spring
  • The upper strut bearing mount
  • The strut shaft seal
Correct. The upper strut bearing lets the strut rotate when you steer. When it wears or seizes, you hear clicking or grinding tied to steering input.
Not quite. The noise is tied to steering motion, which points to the upper strut bearing - the part that lets the strut rotate. Ball joints clunk under load, springs don't make rotational noise, and shaft seals leak fluid but don't click.
Knowledge Check - Question 3 of 3
Why must wheel alignment always be checked after MacPherson strut replacement?
  • Because the wheel bearings need to be repacked
  • Because the strut's position affects camber and other alignment angles
  • Because the ABS system needs to be recalibrated
  • Alignment doesn't need to be checked after strut work
Correct. The strut's mounting locations directly determine camber and caster. Any time you R&R a strut, you should align the vehicle. Some struts even have slotted holes to allow camber adjustment.
Not quite. The strut's position dictates camber angle. Bolting it back into place often shifts the camber slightly even when using the same mounting holes. Alignment is required after every strut R&R.
